MPLADS - A Citizen's Guide
How the public can use, track and question MPLADS works
Note: Compiled from public sources (MoSPI MPLADS Guidelines, PIB releases, mplads.gov.in and secondary
summaries) as of October 2026. This is an unofficial knowledge-base document. For binding rules always refer to the
latest official Guidelines/circulars issued by the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI).
What citizens can and cannot do
Ordinary citizens cannot apply for MPLADS funds directly, and the scheme does not give money to
individuals or families. It pays for community assets, so the way a citizen takes part is by bringing a local
need to the notice of the Member of Parliament who represents their area. Residents of a Lok Sabha
constituency can approach their Lok Sabha Member, and residents of a State can also approach any
elected Rajya Sabha Member from that State. A well-presented request, ideally supported by the
panchayat or local body and describing who will benefit and who will maintain the asset, has a much better
chance of being taken up.
Citizens should also know that the scheme is not meant for private benefit. Requests such as a house for a
family, a personal vehicle or a cash grant fall outside the rules, whereas requests for a school room, a
village road, a public water point or a community hall are the kind of work the scheme was designed for.
Finding information about works
Information on MPLADS works is meant to be public. The eSAKSHI portal has a public dashboard that
shows works recommended by Members, works sanctioned by District Authorities, the progress reported
by implementing agencies and the status of completion. The official MPLADS website also carries
guidelines and circulars. In addition, the District Authority is required to display the list of completed and
ongoing works in its office, and details of completed works may be shown at block, tehsil and panchayat
offices.
Every completed work must carry a plaque at the site. By reading the plaque, a citizen can learn the cost,
the dates and the Member who recommended the work, and can compare these details with what the
portal shows. If the details do not match, or if the plaque is missing, that itself is worth reporting.
Using the Right to Information Act
Under the Right to Information Act, 2005, every citizen has the right to ask for information on any aspect of
MPLADS. This can include which works were recommended by a Member, which were sanctioned or
rejected and why, the cost, the name of the implementing agency, the user agency and the quality of the
finished work. District Authorities are responsible for providing this information in the manner required by
the Act. An application should be addressed to the public information officer in the office of the District
Collector or the concerned department, and should state clearly the period, the constituency and the type
of information sought.
Raising a complaint
If a work is incomplete, of poor quality, delayed beyond its deadline, or if an asset such as an ambulance is
being misused or not used, the first place to complain is the District Authority, since it is responsible for
supervising MPLADS works in the district. The guidelines require public notices with contact numbers to
be displayed in places such as government hospitals and municipal or panchayat offices for ambulances
and hearse vans purchased through the scheme, so that complaints about misuse or non-use can be
lodged.
If the district office does not respond, a citizen can write to the State Nodal Department that supervises
MPLADS in the State, or to the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation in New Delhi, and
may also use the central public grievance system. Complaints are more effective when they give the work
name, location, Member, approximate sanction date and any photographs or documents. The Ministry and
the State Governments conduct inspections and third-party quality checks of works, and complaints can
help direct attention to problem sites.
Why citizen monitoring matters
The scheme depends on several layers of government working together, and delays often arise in the
gaps between them. Citizens who know the timelines, namely 45 days for a reply on a recommendation,
75 days for sanction and generally one year for completion, are better placed to ask the right questions.
They are also better placed to notice when a user agency is not maintaining an asset, which is one of the
commonest reasons a useful structure falls into disrepair.
